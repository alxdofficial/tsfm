Paper summary
Strengths
This paper presents HALO, a heterogeneity-aware, language-aligned IMU foundation model for open-set human activity
recognition. The paper targets two major barriers in mobile/wearable HAR: sensing heterogeneity across datasets and devices,
and limited generalization to unseen activity labels. HALO uses a two-stage training approach. First, it pretrains an IMU
encoder with adaptive-pooling tokenization for variable sampling rates, channel-independent feature extraction for variable
sensor suites, and natural-language sensor conditioning for modality/axis/placement descriptions. Second, it aligns IMU
embeddings with text embeddings using synonym-aware soft contrastive learning, enabling activity prediction via cosine
similarity against arbitrary text label banks. The model is trained on 10 public HAR datasets and evaluated on 7 held-out
datasets, including zero-shot open-set, zero-shot closed-set, and low-label fine-tuning settings.
Adaptive-pooling tokenization addresses variable sampling rates, channel-independent encoding addresses variable sensor
suites, and sensor-description conditioning provides placement/modality context. The language-aligned inference interface is
useful, as swapping label banks at runtime without training a new classifier is a practical capability for open-set HAR.

combination of these pieces for heterogeneous IMU HAR.

framework combines heterogeneity-aware self-supervised pretraining with IMU–text contrastive alignment to support open-set

What, if anything, could convince you to accept this paper?
The paper discusses activities that have “no semantic analog” in the training label set. However, it is difficult to understand
how cosine similarity retrieval over text embeddings can meaningfully recognize such activities if no semantic counterpart
exists during training. The severe out-of-domain results seem to confirm this limitation, as all models collapse in these
settings. On the other hand, some of the simpler label mismatch problems discussed in the paper (e.g., walking vs jogging)
appear solvable through post-processing or label normalization rather than requiring a new foundation model framework.
The paper emphasizes synonym-aware alignment as a major challenge and contribution, but it is unclear whether label
variation in HAR datasets is sufficiently complex to justify this focus. Many label inconsistencies may be relatively easy to map
manually or automatically after prediction. Furthermore, textual similarity may not necessarily correspond to similarity in sensor
dynamics, especially for activities that are semantically related but physically different.
While the reported gains over baselines are consistent, the absolute zero-shot open-set accuracy remains relatively low
(around 42%). It is unclear whether such performance is sufficient for real deployment scenarios.
In Figure 2, all the test datasets appear to lie closely together in space. Shouldn't there be 7 of them, and shouldn't they be
more diverse? Also, the paper uses a fixed benchmark split with specific train and test datasets, but it would be useful to
evaluate additional train/test combinations to better understand generalization behavior.
For datasets without placement annotations (e.g., UCI-HAR provides only “smartphone” without specifying pocket vs. hand),
not knowing the placement wouldn't lead to incorrect predictions? Would it be better not to use that dataset? Have you tested
with and without including such datasets in training and testing?
It is said that HALO uses around 35 million trainable parameters, 10X fewer than MOMENT. Is there a tradeoff? Can you use
more parameters and improve accuracy?
Overall, this is a practically motivated paper with strong experimental effort, but the novelty and key conceptual contribution
need to be clarified much more explicitly. The paper would benefit from a clearer explanation of what is fundamentally new and
a stronger justification for why the identified challenges require architectural solutions rather than simpler post-processing
approaches.

Paper summary
Strengths
The paper proposes HALO, a heterogeneity-aware and language-aligned IMU foundation model for open-set human activity
recognition. HALO addresses two major limitations of existing HAR models: reliance on fixed sensor configurations and poor
generalization to new activity labels. The proposed model has two stages: first, it pretrains an IMU encoder with adaptive-
pooling tokenization, channel-independent processing, and sensor-description conditioning to handle different sampling rates,
channel counts, and sensor placements. Then, it aligns IMU embeddings with text embeddings via synonym-aware contrastive
learning, enabling activity recognition via cosine similarity with a flexible label set. Evaluation on multiple public HAR datasets
shows that it outperforms several strong baselines on the main held-out datasets, especially in zero-shot open-set recognition,
although performance still drops sharply under severe out-of-domain shifts.
−  The paper addresses an important and practical problem in IMU-based HAR. It clearly identifies sensing heterogeneity
and inconsistent label vocabularies as major barriers to building a reusable HAR foundation model.
−  The proposed design is well motivated and technically coherent. Adaptive-pooling tokenization, channel-independent
processing, sensor-text conditioning, and synonym-aware text alignment are all directly connected to the challenges
discussed in the motivation.
−  The evaluation includes broad comparisons with many HAR papers, including both main test datasets and severe out-of-
domain datasets.

Weaknesses
Comments for authors
What, if anything, could convince you to accept this paper?
−  The paper includes useful practical analysis beyond accuracy, which helps readers understand the usefulness and
limitations of HALO.
−  The evaluation protocol is not fully balanced across models, which makes some comparisons harder to interpret. HALO
benefits from native sampling rates and rich channel descriptions, whereas several baselines use 20 Hz-resampled data
and more limited input formats, even though the evaluation includes a partial fairness control.
−  The "open-set" and "open-vocabulary" claims need clearer definition and stronger evidence. In the zero-shot open-set
setting, prediction is made over the 87 training labels, so the setting does not fully demonstrate recognition of genuinely
novel activity names at inference time.
−  HALO still collapses under severe out-of-domain shifts, which weakens the foundation-model claim. On HARTH and VTT-
ConIoT, zero-shot accuracy is near zero for HALO, indicating that the model still struggles with strong sensor-placement,
modality, and activity-distribution shifts.
−  The ablation study is useful but not sufficiently fine-grained. Several major components are introduced, but the paper
mostly ablates broad modules, making it difficult to determine the independent contribution of adaptive pooling, spectral-
temporal tokenization, soft targets, memory queues, synonym augmentation, and the choice of Sentence-BERT.
The paper would be stronger if the comparison protocol were more systematically controlled. The current setup gives each
model the richest input it can consume, which is reasonable from an engineering perspective, but it also makes it difficult to
isolate the contribution of HALOʼs gains to the model architecture, native-rate processing, richer sensor metadata, or the
language-aligned label interface. The fairness control in Table 7 is helpful, but a more complete comparison with feasible,
adaptive baselines that use the same channel sets and sampling rates would be helpful. The paper should also more clearly
discuss how group-based scoring for classifier models and exact string matching for text-aligned models affect the reported
numbers.
The paper should distinguish more carefully between “zero-shot transfer to unseen datasets,” “closed-set recognition with test
labels,” “open-set recognition over training labels,” and “open-vocabulary recognition with truly novel labels.” The current zero-
shot open-set setting appears to use the 87-label training vocabulary, meaning the model cannot output activity labels absent
from training. To better support the open-vocabulary claim, the authors should include an evaluation that includes unseen test
labels, paraphrases, fine-grained labels, and distractor labels. It would also be valuable to report performance separately for
labels with close training-set synonyms and for genuinely novel labels.
The severe out-of-domain results are important, but the current discussion is too brief relative to how strongly they challenge
the paperʼs foundation-model framing. Since HALO nearly collapses in zero-shot on HARTH and VTT-ConIoT, the authors
should provide a more thorough failure analysis, including confusion matrices, examples of the nearest text labels, per-activity
accuracy, and breakdowns by sensor placement, sensor modality, and label coverage.
The paper should also temper its claims by emphasizing that HALO is effective under moderate cross-dataset heterogeneity
but does not yet solve extreme sensor or domain shift. A discussion of possible remedies, such as per-sample sensor
metadata, small calibration sets, domain-adaptive pretraining, or uncertainty-based rejection, would make the limitation more
constructive.
The current ablations show that channel-text conditioning, text augmentation, and signal augmentation matter, but they do not
fully explain why the system works. Additional ablations (e.g., adaptive-pooling tokenizer vs. fixed resampling, temporal-only
vs. spectral-temporal tokenization, channel-independent vs. channel-specific processing) would help demonstrate their
effectiveness. The authors should also report variance over multiple seeds, especially because the ablation runs use a shorter
training schedule than the final model.
−  A more controlled comparison that better isolates the benefit of HALOʼs modeling choices from differences in input
settings, such as native sampling rates, richer channel descriptions, and scoring protocols.
−  The open-vocabulary claim would be more convincing with a direct evaluation using truly unseen activity labels,
paraphrases, fine-grained labels, and distractor labels, rather than mainly relying on labels that overlap semantically with
the training vocabulary.
−  The paper would benefit from a deeper analysis of the severe out-of-domain failures on HARTH and VTT-ConIoT, where
HALO nearly collapses in zero-shot settings despite being framed as a foundation model.
